If you've started exploring your mortgage options in Canada, you've almost certainly encountered the term **stress test**. Formally known as the **OSFI B-20 guideline** (a directive from Canada's Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions), the stress test applies to all federally regulated lenders and requires them to confirm that you could still afford your mortgage payments if interest rates were to rise significantly. It doesn't change the rate you actually pay — it determines the maximum mortgage you're *allowed to borrow*. For buyers in high-cost BC markets, understanding this rule is essential to setting realistic expectations.

---

## How the Stress Test Works

The stress test requires that your lender qualify you at a **higher rate than the one you'll actually pay** — specifically, the greater of these two rates:

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Your Contract Rate + 2%" icon="plus">
    Whatever rate you actually negotiate with your lender, add 2 percentage points. That's your qualifying rate under this test.

    **Example:** If your lender offers a 5-year fixed at **4.89%**, your stress test qualifying rate is **6.89%**.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Bank of Canada Benchmark Rate" icon="bank">
    The minimum floor rate set by the Bank of Canada for mortgage qualification purposes. As of 2024, this floor is **5.25%**. The qualifying rate is whichever of these two options is *higher*.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

In most rate environments, **contract rate + 2% produces the higher qualifying rate** and is therefore the one that applies. The floor of 5.25% only becomes relevant if contract rates drop very low (below approximately 3.25%).

### What Does "Qualifying at a Higher Rate" Mean in Practice?

When your lender stress tests you, they run your income and debt numbers as if your mortgage rate were 6.89% (in the example above) — not 4.89%. This results in a higher theoretical monthly payment, which reduces the maximum mortgage you're approved for.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Without Stress Test">
    **Household income:** $150,000/year  
    **Contract rate used for qualification:** 4.89%  
    **Estimated maximum insured mortgage:** ~$720,000  
    *(Based on standard GDS/TDS ratios, before stress test adjustment)*
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="With Stress Test (at 6.89%)">
    **Household income:** $150,000/year  
    **Qualifying rate:** 6.89% (contract rate 4.89% + 2%)  
    **Estimated maximum insured mortgage:** ~$590,000  
    *(Same income and debts — qualification reduced by ~$130,000)*
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Info>
  The figures above are illustrative estimates based on standard GDS/TDS ratios and a typical insured mortgage scenario. Your actual qualifying amount will depend on your full income picture, existing debts, down payment, property taxes, and lender-specific policies. Always confirm with a licensed mortgage broker.
</Info>

## Who the Stress Test Applies To

### Federally Regulated Lenders

The OSFI B-20 stress test applies to **all federally regulated lenders**, which includes:

- The Big Six chartered banks (RBC, TD, BMO, Scotiabank, CIBC, National Bank)
- Most federally chartered trust companies
- Most monoline mortgage lenders (lenders that only offer mortgages, not full banking services)

If your mortgage is originated through any of these institutions — regardless of whether it's insured or uninsured — the stress test applies.

### BC Credit Unions: A Notable Exception

<Note>
  **BC Credit Unions are provincially regulated**, not federally regulated — which means OSFI's B-20 guideline does not automatically apply to them. Credit unions in British Columbia are regulated under the provincial *Financial Institutions Act* and overseen by the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA). Some BC credit unions apply their own internal mortgage qualification rules (MQR), which may be more flexible than the federal stress test. This can be meaningful for self-employed borrowers, those with non-traditional income, or buyers who narrowly miss federally regulated lender thresholds. However, policies vary by institution — not all credit unions offer materially different qualifying standards, and their rates and product options may differ. Speak with a mortgage broker who works with BC credit unions to explore this path.
</Note>

## Strategies to Maximize Your Qualifying Amount

If the stress test is limiting what you qualify for, there are several legitimate strategies to improve your position:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Add a Co-Borrower or Co-Signor">
    Adding a spouse, partner, parent, or other qualifying individual to your mortgage application combines household incomes, which directly increases your qualifying ceiling. A **co-borrower** has ownership of the property; a **co-signor** (guarantor) supports the application without necessarily being on title. Both approaches strengthen the application, but both individuals' debts are also factored in — so this works best when the added person has strong income and manageable liabilities.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Extend Your Amortization Period">
    A longer amortization produces a lower theoretical monthly payment, which improves your qualifying debt ratios (GDS and TDS). Moving from a 25-year to a 30-year amortization (where eligible) can add tens of thousands of dollars to your qualifying mortgage. See our [Amortization & Terms](/client-guide/amortization-terms) guide for a full breakdown.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pay Down Existing Debts">
    The stress test calculation uses your **Total Debt Service (TDS) ratio** — which includes all monthly debt obligations: mortgage payment, property taxes, heating, and any other debt payments (car loans, lines of credit, credit cards, student loans). Eliminating a car payment or paying down a line of credit before applying can meaningfully improve your ratio and your qualifying amount.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose a Lender with More Flexible Income Treatment">
    Different lenders treat income differently — particularly for self-employed borrowers, commissioned salespeople, rental income earners, and those with non-traditional income sources. Some lenders use **gross income**, others use **net income**; some allow higher add-backs for business owners. A mortgage broker with access to multiple lenders can find the institution whose income policies work best for your situation.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  **Variable rate mortgages are still stress tested at contract rate + 2%.** A common misconception is that choosing a variable rate mortgage — which has a lower starting rate — results in a higher qualifying amount. It does not. Whether your mortgage is fixed or variable, the stress test adds 2% to your *actual contract rate* (or applies the 5.25% floor, whichever is higher). A variable at 5.10% stress tests at 7.10%, which is *higher* than a fixed at 4.89% testing at 6.89%.
</Warning>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Does the stress test apply at renewal if I stay with my lender?">
    **Generally, no — for uninsured mortgages.** As of June 2021, OSFI clarified that federally regulated lenders are not required to re-apply the stress test when an existing uninsured mortgage borrower *renews with the same lender* (a "straight renewal"). This means if your income has changed or rates have risen since you first qualified, renewing in place is typically seamless. 

    However, if you **switch lenders at renewal** — even without refinancing — the new lender must re-qualify you, including the stress test. This is an important consideration: the best renewal rate may come from a new lender, but if your financial picture has changed significantly since your original qualification, you'll need to ensure you can pass the stress test at the new lender's qualifying rate.

    For **insured mortgages** (less than 20% down, backed by CMHC, Sagen, or Canada Guaranty), the rules may differ — confirm with your broker.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Does the stress test apply at credit unions in BC?">
    It depends on the specific credit union. BC credit unions are **not bound by OSFI's B-20 guidelines** since they are provincially regulated. However, many BC credit unions voluntarily apply similar or equivalent qualification standards for prudent lending practices. Some credit unions do apply a modified mortgage qualifying rate (MQR) that may be slightly more lenient than the federal standard, particularly for certain borrower types. The extent of any flexibility varies by institution, their internal risk appetite, and current regulatory guidance from the BCFSA. A mortgage broker familiar with BC credit unions can identify whether a credit union pathway is appropriate for your situation and which institutions currently offer the most flexibility.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How often does the benchmark qualifying rate change?">
    The **Bank of Canada benchmark rate** (the 5.25% floor used in the stress test) is formally reviewed and published each week, but in practice it doesn't change frequently. It is based on the mode of the posted 5-year fixed rates from Canada's six major banks. OSFI also reviews and can adjust the stress test floor rate when market conditions warrant. During 2020–2021, it was temporarily lowered to 4.79% and subsequently raised back up. Any changes are announced publicly and typically receive significant media coverage. When in doubt, check the current qualifying floor directly with your mortgage broker before running qualifying scenarios.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

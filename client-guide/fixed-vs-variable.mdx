Choosing between a fixed and variable rate mortgage is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make in your mortgage journey. It affects everything from your monthly budget to the total interest you pay over the life of your loan — and getting it wrong (especially if you need to break the mortgage early) can be costly. This guide breaks down how each type works, how rates are set, and how to think about the right choice for your situation in British Columbia.

## Fixed vs. Variable at a Glance

| Feature | Fixed Rate | Variable Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Rate movement | Locked in for the full term | Moves with the Bank of Canada prime rate |
| Payment stability | Consistent throughout the term | May fluctuate (or static with adjustable balance) |
| Rate sensitivity | No exposure to rate hikes during the term | Payment or amortization affected by every rate move |
| Prepayment penalty | IRD or 3-months interest (whichever is greater) | Typically 3-months interest only |
| Penalty predictability | Can be very large and hard to estimate in advance | Simple, formulaic, and easy to calculate |
| Starting rate | Typically higher than the initial variable rate | Typically lower than a comparable fixed rate |
| Best for | Predictability, budget certainty, rate risk aversion | Rate flexibility, tolerance for fluctuation, near-peak rate environments |
| Typical term | 1–5 years | 3–5 years |

## How Fixed Rates Are Priced

Fixed mortgage rates in Canada are primarily driven by the **Government of Canada (GoC) bond market** — not the Bank of Canada's overnight rate. Here's how the chain works:

1. **Bond yields move** based on investor expectations about economic growth, inflation, and future interest rate direction.
2. **Lenders add a spread** on top of the comparable bond yield (usually the 5-year GoC bond for a 5-year fixed) to cover their cost of funds, operating costs, and profit margin.
3. **Your rate is quoted** as a discount off the lender's posted rate, based on your credit profile and the lender's current promotions.

This is why fixed rates can rise or fall even when the Bank of Canada hasn't moved its overnight rate — bond markets are always pricing in future expectations. When economic uncertainty rises and investors buy bonds (pushing yields down), fixed mortgage rates often follow.

<Info>
  Fixed rates are typically quoted for 1, 2, 3, or 5-year terms. The 5-year fixed has historically been the most popular mortgage product in Canada due to its balance of predictability and competitive pricing.
</Info>

## How Variable Rates Are Priced

Variable mortgage rates flow directly from the **Bank of Canada's overnight lending rate**:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Bank of Canada sets the overnight rate">
    The Bank of Canada meets roughly eight times per year and announces changes (or holds) to its overnight rate — the rate at which major banks lend to each other overnight.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Chartered banks set their prime rate">
    Canada's major banks (RBC, TD, BMO, Scotiabank, CIBC, National Bank) set their **prime rate**, which historically sits 2.20% above the overnight rate. When the overnight rate moves, prime moves in lockstep.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Your variable rate = prime ± a discount or premium">
    Your mortgage is priced as a **discount** (e.g., prime − 0.70%) or, in some rate environments, a **premium** (e.g., prime + 0.25%) relative to prime. That spread is locked in for your term — only the prime rate itself changes.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Example:** If prime is 5.95% and your variable rate is prime − 0.60%, your current rate is **5.35%**. If the Bank of Canada cuts overnight by 0.25%, prime drops to 5.70% and your rate becomes **5.10%** — automatically, with no refinancing required.

## Adjustable Rate vs. Variable Rate (Static Payment)

This is a distinction many borrowers in BC are surprised to learn about — not all variable rate mortgages behave the same way.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Adjustable Rate Mortgage (ARM)">
    In an **Adjustable Rate Mortgage**, your **monthly payment changes** whenever the prime rate moves.

    - When prime goes up, your payment goes up (more of each payment covers the higher interest cost).
    - When prime goes down, your payment goes down.
    - Your amortization schedule stays on track regardless of rate movements.
    - **Who this suits:** Borrowers who can tolerate payment fluctuation and want to always be paying down principal at the expected pace.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Variable Rate Mortgage (Static Payment)">
    In a **Variable Rate Mortgage with a static payment**, your **monthly payment stays the same**, but the *split between principal and interest* changes as rates move.

    - When prime goes up, more of your payment goes to interest and less to principal — your amortization extends.
    - When prime falls, more goes to principal and your amortization shortens.
    - Some lenders have a **trigger rate** — if prime rises enough that your entire payment covers only interest, the lender may require a payment increase or a lump sum.
    - **Who this suits:** Borrowers who need payment certainty but still want to benefit from potential rate drops.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Warning>
  If you have a static-payment variable mortgage, ask your lender what your **trigger rate** is. If the prime rate rises to that level, your lender can require an immediate payment increase or lump-sum payment. Several borrowers were caught off guard during the 2022–2023 rate hiking cycle.
</Warning>

## When Does Variable Win?

<Tip>
  Historically, borrowers with variable rate mortgages have paid less total interest over a full 5-year term than those who chose fixed — in studies covering the past 25+ years of Canadian mortgage data, variable outperformed in the majority of 5-year windows. However, this is not a guarantee: the 2022–2023 rate cycle was a stark reminder that variable rates can rise quickly and significantly. Variable tends to work best when rates are at or near a peak and the next move is likely downward.
</Tip>

## Breaking Your Mortgage Early: The Penalty Difference

This is where the fixed vs. variable decision can have the biggest financial impact — and it's often overlooked.

### Fixed Rate Penalty: Interest Rate Differential (IRD)

If you break a fixed-rate mortgage early (to refinance, sell your home, or port to a new property), your lender will charge the **greater of**:
- **3 months' interest**, OR
- **The Interest Rate Differential (IRD)** — the difference between your contract rate and the lender's current rate for the remaining term, multiplied by your outstanding balance and remaining months.

<Warning>
  IRD penalties on fixed mortgages can be shockingly large — sometimes **$15,000 to $30,000 or more** on a typical BC mortgage — especially when your contract rate is significantly higher than current rates. Always calculate the potential penalty before committing to a fixed term if there's any chance you may need to break it early.
</Warning>

### Variable Rate Penalty: 3 Months' Interest

Variable rate mortgages almost universally use a simple **3-months' interest** penalty — far more predictable and typically much smaller than an IRD penalty. For a $500,000 mortgage at 5.35%, that's roughly **$6,688** — still significant, but calculable in advance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Can I convert my variable rate mortgage to a fixed rate mid-term?">
    Yes — virtually all major lenders and most monolines allow you to **convert your variable rate mortgage to a fixed rate** at any point during your term, without triggering a prepayment penalty. The catch: you'll be converting to the lender's *current posted fixed rate* for a term at least as long as your remaining term, which may not always be the most competitive rate available. If you're considering a conversion, compare the offered rate to what other lenders are currently offering and factor in whether you'd rather pay a small variable penalty to refinance elsewhere.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What happens to my variable rate mortgage when the prime rate changes?">
    When the Bank of Canada changes its overnight rate, Canadian banks typically adjust their prime rate within **one business day**. Your variable mortgage rate (and payment or amortization, depending on your product type) updates at your next payment cycle — usually the following month. You'll receive a statement or notice from your lender showing the updated rate. There is no paperwork, no approval process, and no fees — it happens automatically under the terms of your mortgage contract.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

Two of the most misunderstood concepts in Canadian mortgages are **amortization** and **term** — and they are not the same thing. Confusing them can lead to surprises at renewal time, unexpected penalties, or a payment structure that doesn't fit your financial goals. Understanding both gives you real control over how much you pay each month, how quickly you build equity, and how much interest you pay over the life of your mortgage.

---

## The Essential Distinction

<Note>
  **Amortization Period** — The *total* length of time it would take to fully pay off your mortgage if you made every scheduled payment and your interest rate never changed. In Canada, the most common amortization is **25 years**, though options range from 5 to 30 years. This is the number that drives your monthly payment amount.
</Note>

<Note>
  **Mortgage Term** — The length of your *current contract* with your lender. This is typically 1 to 5 years. At the end of your term, your mortgage comes up for **renewal** — you negotiate a new rate and term for the remaining amortization balance. Most Canadians renew their mortgage 4–5 times before fully paying it off.
</Note>

Think of it this way: your **amortization is the finish line**, and your **term is the current leg of the race**. Each time you renew, you're starting a new leg — ideally at a better pace (rate) than the last.

---

## How Amortization Affects Your Payments and Total Interest

The longer your amortization, the lower your monthly payment — but the more total interest you pay. Here's a clear comparison at a 5% interest rate on a $100,000 mortgage balance:

| Amortization | Monthly Payment (per $100K at 5%) | Total Interest Paid |
|---|---|---|
| 20 years | ~$660 | ~$58,500 |
| 25 years | ~$585 | ~$75,500 |
| 30 years | ~$537 | ~$93,000 |

The difference between a 20-year and a 30-year amortization on a $100,000 balance is approximately **$34,500 in extra interest** — for a $500,000 mortgage, that's over **$170,000 in additional interest** over the life of the loan. The tradeoff is a lower monthly obligation that may be necessary for cash flow, especially in high-price BC markets.

<Tip>
  If you can comfortably afford the higher payment, choosing a **shorter amortization** is one of the highest-return financial decisions you can make. Every dollar of extra principal payment made early in your mortgage saves you multiple dollars in future interest. Even moving from 25 years to 22 years can save tens of thousands of dollars over the life of the mortgage.
</Tip>

<Note>
  **30-Year Amortization for First-Time Buyers (New Builds):** As of August 1, 2024, the federal government expanded access to **30-year amortization on insured mortgages** (those with less than 20% down payment) for first-time homebuyers purchasing a newly constructed home. This was introduced to improve affordability in high-cost markets like Metro Vancouver. Confirm current eligibility rules with your mortgage broker, as policies may be updated.
</Note>

## Mortgage Term Options in Canada

Your term determines how long you're locked into your current interest rate and lender contract. Choosing the right term involves weighing rate certainty, your personal timeline, and where you think rates are headed.

| Term | Best Use Case |
|---|---|
| 1-year | Expecting a rate drop in the near future; plans to sell or refinance within 12 months; willing to re-evaluate frequently |
| 2-year | Short-term rate certainty without committing to a longer product; useful in a declining rate environment |
| 3-year | Balanced option — moderate security with the ability to reassess in a reasonable timeframe |
| 5-year fixed | Most popular term in Canada — maximizes payment predictability; ideal for buyers who value stability |
| 5-year variable | Potential for rate savings over the term; best when rates are elevated and expected to decrease |

<Info>
  The **5-year fixed** has historically been Canada's most popular mortgage term, partly because insured (CMHC-backed) mortgage statistics have long been anchored to it, and partly because many borrowers prefer knowing exactly what their payments will be for five years. However, shorter terms have gained popularity during periods of rising or elevated rates when borrowers prefer to keep their options open.
</Info>

## What Happens at Renewal

At the end of your mortgage term, you reach your **renewal date**. At this point:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Your lender sends a renewal offer">
    Typically 21–30 days before your term ends, your current lender will send a renewal offer with proposed new rates and terms. These initial offers are rarely their best — they're counting on your inertia.
  </Step>
  <Step title="You compare the market">
    Your renewal is one of the best opportunities to **renegotiate or switch lenders** with no penalty. Competing offers from other lenders or through a mortgage broker can often beat your existing lender's renewal offer by a meaningful margin.
  </Step>
  <Step title="You choose a new rate and term">
    Select the rate type (fixed or variable) and term length that best suits your current financial situation and outlook. Your amortization simply continues — if you had 22 years left, you now have a new term within that 22-year window.
  </Step>
  <Step title="You sign the renewal agreement">
    If staying with the same lender, renewal is typically paperwork only — no new appraisal, no re-qualification in most cases. If switching lenders, you'll go through a new application process (income verification, credit check, property appraisal).
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  Don't let your mortgage renew automatically without reviewing the market. Lenders are required to provide renewal offers in advance, but if you miss the window and do nothing, your mortgage will typically roll into a posted rate that is almost certainly higher than what's available elsewhere. Set a calendar reminder **6 months before your renewal date** to begin shopping.
</Warning>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Can I change my amortization period at renewal?">
    Yes — renewal is one of the key moments when you can **adjust your amortization**. If you want to pay your mortgage off faster, you can shorten your amortization (this will increase your monthly payment). If you're going through a period of financial pressure, some lenders will allow you to extend your amortization back toward the original period, which reduces your payment — though this increases total interest paid. Note that re-extending amortization on an insured mortgage has regulatory limits; speak with your broker about what's available for your specific situation.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Is a longer amortization always worse?">
    Not necessarily — it depends entirely on your financial situation and goals. A longer amortization means a lower required monthly payment, which can:

    - **Improve cash flow** for households with variable income or high living costs
    - **Free up capital** for investing in assets that may outperform the interest cost of carrying the mortgage longer
    - **Reduce financial stress** and the risk of missing payments during difficult periods

    The "cost" of a longer amortization is real — you will pay more total interest — but if the freed-up cash is deployed wisely (RRSP contributions, TFSA investing, business reinvestment), the net financial outcome can be comparable or even favourable. This is a conversation worth having with both your mortgage broker and financial advisor.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

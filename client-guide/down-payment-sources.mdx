In Canada, the minimum down payment required depends on the purchase price of the property. For homes under $1,000,000, buyers can access **CMHC-insured (high-ratio) mortgages** with as little as 5% down — but mortgage default insurance applies. For homes at $1,000,000 or more, a minimum of **20% is required** and the mortgage is uninsured (conventional). Where your down payment comes from matters just as much as how much you have — Canadian lenders and mortgage insurers have strict rules about which sources are acceptable and what documentation is required to prove them.

## Minimum Down Payment by Purchase Price

| Purchase Price | Minimum Down Payment |
|---|---|
| Up to $500,000 | 5% of purchase price |
| $500,001 – $999,999 | 5% on first $500,000 + 10% on the remainder |
| $1,000,000 and above | 20% (uninsured — mortgage insurance not available) |

> **Example:** For a $750,000 home, the minimum down payment is ($500,000 × 5%) + ($250,000 × 10%) = $25,000 + $25,000 = **$50,000**.

## Accepted Down Payment Sources

Canadian lenders accept a range of down payment sources, provided you can document the origin of the funds. Below is a breakdown of the most common sources and what each requires.

<AccordionGroup>

  <Accordion title="Personal Savings — Chequing & Savings Accounts">
    The most straightforward and universally accepted source. Lenders require **90 days of bank statements** for all accounts used toward the down payment. The statements must show:

    - Your name and account number
    - A consistent balance history demonstrating the funds have been accumulated over time
    - No large, unexplained deposits within the 90-day window (these trigger additional scrutiny)

    **Documentation required:**
    - Most recent 90 days of full bank statements (all pages)
    - If a large deposit appears, a written explanation and supporting documentation showing its source

    Funds in high-interest savings accounts (HISAs), Tax-Free Savings Accounts (TFSAs), or GICs all qualify under this category, subject to the same 90-day history requirement.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="RRSP Home Buyers' Plan (HBP)">
    The **Home Buyers' Plan (HBP)** allows first-time home buyers to withdraw up to **$35,000 per person** (or $70,000 per couple) from their Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) tax-free to use toward a qualifying home purchase.

    **Key rules:**
    - You must be a **first-time home buyer** (defined as not having owned a principal residence in the current year or the preceding four calendar years)
    - Funds must have been in the RRSP for **at least 90 days** before withdrawal
    - The withdrawn amount must be repaid to your RRSP over **15 years**, beginning two years after the year of withdrawal — if you miss a year's repayment, that amount is added to your taxable income

    **Documentation required:**
    - RRSP account statements (90-day history)
    - CRA Form T1036 (Home Buyers' Plan Request to Withdraw Funds from an RRSP)

    <Note>
      If both partners are first-time buyers, each can withdraw up to $35,000 from their respective RRSPs — for a combined total of $70,000 toward the down payment.
    </Note>
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Gifted Funds — Family Gifts">
    Lenders accept gifted down payments provided the gift comes from an **immediate family member**. Accepted family members typically include parents, grandparents, siblings, or children. Gifts from friends, extended family, or employers are generally not accepted.

    **The gift must be a true gift — not a loan.** The donor must confirm in writing that no repayment is expected or required.

    **Documentation required:**
    - A **signed gift letter** from the donor, confirming: the dollar amount, the relationship to the borrower, that the funds are a non-repayable gift, and the source of the gift funds
    - Bank statement from the **donor** showing the funds being withdrawn
    - Bank statement from the **borrower** confirming the deposit of gifted funds

    <Warning>
      If the gift letter language implies any repayment obligation — or if lender statements suggest repayment is expected — the funds will be treated as a loan and may disqualify or reduce your eligible down payment.
    </Warning>
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Sale of an Existing Property">
    If you are selling a current home and using the net proceeds toward your next purchase, this is one of the most straightforward sources to document — provided the timing aligns.

    **Documentation required:**
    - A copy of the **accepted offer to purchase** (MLS listing agreement or signed contract of purchase and sale) on the property being sold
    - **Closing/completion statement** from your lawyer or notary showing net proceeds
    - If the sale hasn't yet completed, a lawyer's letter confirming expected net proceeds may be accepted

    Lenders will verify that the proceeds are sufficient and that any existing mortgage on the sold property has been or will be discharged at closing.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Investments & Non-Registered Accounts">
    Funds held in non-registered investment accounts — including brokerage accounts, mutual funds, stocks, bonds, or ETFs — are an acceptable source of down payment, but require additional steps.

    Because investment values fluctuate, lenders want to confirm the **liquidation value** and verify a 90-day history.

    **Documentation required:**
    - 90 days of investment account statements
    - Evidence that funds have been liquidated (transferred to a bank account) before closing, or a commitment to liquidate by the closing date
    - If the account is jointly held, a letter from the co-holder confirming the borrower's entitlement to the funds

    <Note>
      If you hold assets in a TFSA and plan to use them, ensure funds are withdrawn and deposited into a bank account before the lender's verification date. TFSA re-contribution room is only restored on January 1 of the following year.
    </Note>
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Proceeds from a Business (Self-Employed Borrowers)">
    Self-employed borrowers may use business savings or retained earnings as down payment, but additional documentation is required to demonstrate that the withdrawal won't impair the business — and to satisfy anti-money laundering requirements.

    **Documentation required:**
    - 90 days of **business bank account statements**
    - Evidence of the transfer from the business account to a personal account
    - Most recent **Notice of Assessment (NOA)** and T1 General from CRA
    - Financial statements for the business (may be required if the amount is significant)
    - A letter explaining the nature of the withdrawal and confirming it does not negatively affect business operations

    Some lenders may require a letter from an accountant confirming the legitimacy and availability of the funds.
  </Accordion>

</AccordionGroup>

## The 90-Day Seasoning Rule

<Note>
  **What is "seasoning"?** Most lenders and mortgage insurers (CMHC, Sagen, Canada Guaranty) require that down payment funds have been in the borrower's account for a minimum of **90 days** before the mortgage application. This rule — commonly called the "seasoning" requirement — is designed to verify that funds are genuinely the borrower's own accumulated savings and were not borrowed shortly before the application. Any large deposit appearing within the 90-day window will need to be explained and sourced with documentation.
</Note>

## Borrowed Down Payments

<Warning>
  **Borrowed down payments are generally not permitted for insured (high-ratio) mortgages.** If your down payment is less than 20% and the mortgage requires CMHC or other default insurance, you cannot use funds borrowed from a line of credit, personal loan, credit card advance, or any other debt product as your down payment. Lenders will review your liabilities and credit report — undisclosed borrowed funds can result in mortgage fraud allegations. For **conventional mortgages (20%+ down)**, some lenders may allow a portion of the down payment to come from a secured line of credit, but this is lender-specific and the debt must be included in your debt service ratio calculations.
</Warning>

Helping someone qualify can be generous, but co-signing is a real financial obligation—not just lending your name. Understand the documents and what you could afford if the other borrower cannot pay.

## What a co-signer takes on

A co-borrower or co-signer commonly shares responsibility for the debt. Depending on the agreement, the lender may seek the full unpaid amount from you, not just your imagined share. Ownership on title, debt liability and who makes payments are different questions. Do not assume one automatically answers the others.

## A guarantor is not automatically low-risk

A guarantee is a different legal arrangement, but its precise terms matter. Some guarantees allow direct recourse or waive protections. Do not assume the lender must first exhaust every remedy against the main borrower, that the guarantee cannot affect your borrowing capacity, or that it has no credit consequences. Have an independent legal adviser explain the actual agreement before signing.

## Your own plans may be affected

The obligation may be considered when you later apply for your own mortgage or other credit—even if every payment has been made on time. Missed payments or enforcement may affect credit and finances. Property ownership can also create tax, estate or first-time buyer implications. These deserve separate professional advice.

- Could you make the payments if needed?
- Could this interfere with your own renewal, purchase or retirement plans?
- How will you learn promptly if a payment is missed?
- What happens if a borrower dies, becomes disabled or the relationship changes?

## Agree on a practical monitoring plan

Discuss payment access, communication, insurance and what to do at the first sign of trouble. A private agreement between family members can clarify expectations, but it does not override the lender’s rights unless the lender agrees. Independent advice helps protect the relationship as well as the finances.

## Getting removed later

A co-signer is not automatically released at renewal or when the other borrower’s income improves. The lender generally must approve a release or replacement financing, which may require fresh qualification and legal work. Selling or refinancing can involve costs and penalties. Make an exit plan, but do not treat a hoped-for release date as guaranteed.

- Ask Matt what the lender requires for a future release.
- Put review dates on the calendar.
- Do not sign until you understand the worst-case obligation.

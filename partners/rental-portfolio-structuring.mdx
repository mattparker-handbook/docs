Investment property mortgages involve a layer of complexity that goes well beyond a straightforward purchase. Every existing rental property in a client's portfolio affects their TDS calculation, their lender eligibility, and the rate tier available to them. Getting the structuring right — which lender, which product, how rental income is counted, and how the portfolio is organized — can meaningfully change the economics of a deal. This guide gives Realtors and Financial Planners a working framework for investment property files before they come to us.

## Rental Income Treatment by Lender Type

How a lender treats rental income from investment properties is one of the most consequential variables in investment mortgage structuring. The same property with the same rent can produce dramatically different qualifying outcomes depending on which lender is used.

| Lender Type | Rental Income % Applied | How It's Used | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big Banks — Insured Programs | 50% of gross monthly rents | Offset against rental property PITH | Applies where mortgage is insurable; common for first investment property |
| Big Banks — Conventional Programs | 80% of gross monthly rents | Offset or add-to-income (varies by bank) | Some banks allow 100% on a rental offset basis for conventional files |
| Monoline Lenders | 50–80% of gross monthly rents | Rental offset | Varies significantly by program and lender; confirm at time of submission |
| Alternative / B Lenders | 100% of gross rents | Full rental offset or income inclusion | Higher rates and fees; useful for clients where A lender rental treatment creates TDS issues |

**Rental offset vs. add-to-income:** Under a rental offset model, the rental income reduces the TDS impact of the investment property's mortgage payment — it offsets debt rather than adding income. Under an add-to-income model, the qualifying income figure itself increases. Both can produce similar qualifying outcomes, but the mechanics interact differently with a client's overall TDS picture.

## Minimum Requirements for Investment Properties

Investment properties in Canada are uninsurable — CMHC, Sagen, and Canada Guaranty do not insure investor-owned properties. This shapes the baseline requirements for every investment purchase:

- **Minimum 20% down payment** — no exceptions; all investment property mortgages are conventional
- **Owner-occupied principal residence confirmed** — the borrower must have and maintain a principal residence; lenders verify this as part of the application
- **TDS management** — each additional rental property adds mortgage debt to the TDS calculation; the rental income offsets some or all of it, but the net effect must stay within lender thresholds
- **Credit score of 680+** — most A lenders require a minimum 680 for investment properties; some set the bar at 700 or 720 for portfolio files
- **Stable income sufficient to carry the portfolio** — lenders want to see that the borrower's personal income can service the total debt load independently of rental income, particularly for portfolio files

## Portfolio Lenders vs. Traditional Lenders

<Accordion title="When Does Portfolio (Blanket) Mortgage Financing Make Sense?">
  Traditional residential mortgage lenders — banks and monolines — are typically structured to finance individual properties under standard residential mortgage guidelines. As a client's portfolio grows, they may encounter friction: lenders limiting total residential mortgages, concentration risk policies, or qualification challenges created by multiple mortgage payments in the TDS calculation.

  Portfolio lenders (sometimes called blanket mortgage lenders or commercial real estate lenders) finance multiple properties under a single mortgage facility. This can simplify cash flow management, unlock equity across the portfolio, and allow continued acquisition when traditional residential lending has reached its ceiling.

  **When the shift to portfolio financing typically makes sense:**
  - The client owns **5 or more doors** (individual units, not necessarily buildings)
  - The portfolio has significant equity and the client wants to unlock it efficiently
  - The client is acquiring commercial or mixed-use properties alongside residential
  - Traditional lender TDS qualification is no longer workable and the income picture hasn't changed
  - The client is building toward a portfolio that will be managed as a business, not a passive investment

  Portfolio financing comes with different underwriting standards — the property cash flow and appraisal value carry more weight than the borrower's personal income — and typically higher rates than residential products. It is a tool for the right client at the right stage of portfolio growth.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="The Trigger: When Does Residential Become Commercial?">
  In Canada, properties with **5 or more self-contained residential units** on a single title are generally classified as commercial multi-family rather than residential. This shifts financing from the NHA (National Housing Act) residential framework to commercial lending, with different qualification standards, amortization periods, and rate structures.

  Some clients build portfolios of multiple single-family or small multi-family properties to stay under this threshold; others move deliberately into the commercial space as portfolio size grows. Either approach is valid — but the financing implications are material and should be factored into the client's long-term acquisition plan.
</Accordion>

## Common Structuring Pitfalls

<Accordion title="Concentration Risk With a Single Lender">
  Many investors place all their properties with a single lender for convenience. Lenders are aware of this and apply concentration risk policies — at some point, a lender will decline to add more exposure to a single borrower's portfolio, regardless of how clean the file looks. Distributing properties across multiple lenders maintains access to credit and reduces the risk that one lender's policy change affects the entire portfolio.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Not Separating Business and Personal Credit">
  Investment property mortgages secured on the borrower's personal credit affect their personal credit bureau and TDS ratios. As portfolios grow, this can constrain the borrower's ability to qualify for a primary residence refinance, a HELOC, or other personal financing. Structuring investments through a properly capitalized holding company — where the lender is willing to lend to the corporation — can preserve the owner's personal credit capacity. This requires careful planning and legal/tax advice; bring us in early when a client is considering this transition.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Over-Leveraging Before a Rate Renewal Cycle">
  Investment properties acquired at low rates with high leverage can become cash-flow negative at renewal if rates have increased materially. Clients who built portfolios during the low-rate environment of 2020–2022 are experiencing exactly this challenge. When advising clients on new acquisitions, build the analysis at current and stress-tested rates — not the rate they're hoping for at renewal. We can provide a portfolio stress-test analysis on request.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Misunderstanding Subject Property Rental Income">
  When a client is purchasing an investment property, the rental income from that specific property — the subject of the new mortgage — can sometimes be used to offset the new mortgage payment in the lender's TDS calculation. However, if the property is vacant or the income is unconfirmed, some lenders will not apply any offset. Confirming the rental status and having lease documentation ready before submission avoids surprises during underwriting.
</Accordion>

<Note>
  **BC Foreign Buyer Rules and Their Impact on Investor Clients**

  BC has implemented several measures affecting non-Canadian investors in the residential real estate market. The federal **Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act** (foreign buyer ban) restricts non-Canadians from purchasing residential property in Canada, with certain exemptions — including work permit holders meeting specific criteria, international students in certain circumstances, and properties in areas not subject to the ban.

  Additionally, BC's **Speculation and Vacancy Tax (SVT)** applies to vacant residential properties in designated areas and includes different rate tiers for BC residents, other Canadians, and foreign owners. BC's **Foreign Buyers Tax** (Additional Property Transfer Tax) applies an additional PTT on residential properties purchased by foreign nationals in specified regions.

  Any investor client who is not a Canadian citizen or permanent resident should have their eligibility and tax exposure reviewed by a qualified BC real estate lawyer before proceeding. We can facilitate this referral if needed.
</Note>

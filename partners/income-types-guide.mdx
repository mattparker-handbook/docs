---
title: "How Lenders Assess Different Income Types in Canada"
sidebarTitle: "Income Types"
description: "How Canadian lenders calculate qualifying income for salaried, commission, self-employed, rental, pension, and investment income borrowers in BC."
---

One of the most common sources of surprise in mortgage qualification is how lenders treat income. Not all income is created equal: lenders apply different calculation methods, averaging rules, and documentation requirements depending on how a borrower earns their money. The income figure a client puts on their pre-approval form is rarely the same number the lender uses to calculate their qualifying amount. Use this guide to understand — and anticipate — how your clients' income types will be assessed.

<Note>
  **Gross-Up for Non-Taxable Income:** Several types of income are non-taxable or tax-advantaged in Canada. Many lenders will **gross up** these income types by **25%** when calculating qualifying income, effectively treating $1,000 of non-taxable income as $1,250 of qualifying income. Income types commonly eligible for grossing up include: CPP disability benefits, workers' compensation, certain child and family benefit payments, and disability insurance income. Confirm gross-up treatment with us on a file-by-file basis, as lender policies vary.
</Note>

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Salaried / Hourly (T4)">
    **How Lenders Calculate It**

    Salaried and standard hourly (T4-employed) income is the most straightforward income type for mortgage qualification. Lenders use the base employment income at 100% without averaging, provided it is confirmed in the employment documentation.

    **What's included at 100%:**
    - Base salary (annual, confirmed on employment letter)
    - Guaranteed hourly wages (calculated as hourly rate × guaranteed hours per week × 52 ÷ 12)

    **What requires 2-year averaging:**
    - Overtime pay — unless the employer confirms in writing that overtime is guaranteed and ongoing
    - Shift differentials — treated similarly to overtime
    - Bonus income — included only if the employment letter confirms the bonus is guaranteed; otherwise averaged over 2 years from the NOA/T4

    **Documentation required:**
    - Current employment letter (on letterhead, signed, confirming position, tenure, and salary)
    - Most recent T4 (or last 2 years if bonus/overtime is being included)
    - Most recent Notice of Assessment (NOA) confirming CRA-filed income

    **Notes for Realtors and Financial Planners:**
    A salaried employee who recently received a raise should provide the new employment letter confirming the current salary — lenders will use the higher current figure, not the lower historical average. Probationary employees (less than 3 months with current employer) may face lender restrictions.
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Commission / Variable (Full Disclosure)">
    **How Lenders Calculate It**

    Commission-based and variable income earners are assessed on a 2-year average, reflecting the lender's need to account for income variability. This applies to real estate agents, mortgage brokers, salespeople, and others whose income fluctuates by pay period.

    **Calculation method:**
    - 2-year average of total employment income as reported on Line 15000 of the NOA
    - If income is **declining year-over-year**, most lenders use the **lower year** — not the average
    - If income is increasing year-over-year, lenders typically use the 2-year average

    **Documentation required:**
    - 2 years of NOAs
    - 2 years of T1 Generals (full tax return, not just T4 slips)
    - 2 years of T4s and/or T4A slips where applicable
    - Current employment confirmation or business registration

    **Notes for Realtors and Financial Planners:**
    A client who earned $90,000 last year but only $70,000 the year before qualifies on $80,000. A client who earned $90,000 last year but $110,000 the year before qualifies on $90,000 — the lower figure. Declining income is a common deal complication; flag it early.
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Self-Employed (Sole Proprietor)">
    **How Lenders Calculate It**

    Sole proprietors and unincorporated self-employed borrowers file business income on their personal tax return using a T2125 (Statement of Business or Professional Activities). Lenders assess qualifying income based on the net business income reported on the T1 General after allowable deductions — not gross revenue. Because sole proprietors often claim significant expenses to reduce taxable income, the income available for mortgage qualification is frequently lower than the client expects.

    **Calculation method:**
    - 2-year average of net self-employment income as reported on Line 13500 (self-employment income) or Line 13700 (professional income) of the T1 General
    - If income is **declining year-over-year**, most lenders use the **lower year** — not the 2-year average
    - Some lenders permit limited **income add-backs** for non-cash deductions (e.g., CCA / depreciation claimed on the T2125) that reduced reported income without representing an actual cash outflow

    **Documentation required:**
    - 2 years of T1 Generals (including the T2125 schedule)
    - 2 years of NOAs confirming CRA-assessed income
    - Business registration or GST/HST registration (to confirm the business is active)
    - 6–12 months of business bank statements may be required at some lenders

    **Notes for Realtors and Financial Planners:**
    Sole proprietors who aggressively write off business expenses — home office, vehicle, meals, travel — often find their line 15000 income is too low to qualify at an A lender. Unlike incorporated business owners, there is no corporate tax return to demonstrate business health; qualification rests entirely on the personal T1. Refer these clients early so we can assess the usable qualifying income and identify the most appropriate lender channel before an offer is live.
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Self-Employed (Incorporated)">
    **How Lenders Calculate It**

    Self-employed borrowers who operate through a corporation require specialized assessment. Lenders focus on the personal income withdrawn from the corporation — not the corporation's gross revenue. How income is structured (salary vs. dividends) has significant implications for qualification.

    **Calculation method:**
    - 2-year average of income reported on the owner's personal T1 General (Line 15000)
    - Salary drawn from the corporation is treated similarly to T4 employment income
    - Dividends are included but may be treated differently at some lenders
    - Some lenders permit **income add-backs**, particularly CCA (Capital Cost Allowance / depreciation) where it is a non-cash deduction that reduced reported income

    **Documentation required:**
    - 2 years of personal T1 Generals
    - 2 years of personal NOAs
    - 2 years of corporate T2 returns (at many A lenders)
    - Articles of incorporation or business registration
    - Accountant's letter confirming ownership percentage and ongoing operation

    **Notes for Realtors and Financial Planners:**
    Business owners who aggressively minimize personal income for tax purposes often find that their on-paper income is too low to qualify at A lenders. Proactive structuring — reviewed with the client's accountant — can meaningfully improve qualification in subsequent tax years. Refer early so we can identify the qualifying income before a deal is live.
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Rental Income">
    **How Lenders Calculate It**

    Rental income from investment properties is treated as an offset to housing costs rather than straightforward qualifying income at most lenders. The percentage of gross rental income that a lender will apply depends on lender type and whether the mortgage is insured or conventional.

    **Calculation methods (vary by lender):**
    - **Rental offset (most common):** 50–80% of gross monthly rents is offset against the PITH of the rental property, reducing TDS impact
    - **Add-to-income (some lenders):** A portion of net rental income is added to qualifying income
    - **Subject property rental offset:** Where the client is buying a property with a secondary suite, rental income from the suite may offset a portion of the new mortgage payment

    **Documentation required:**
    - Current signed lease agreements (or market rent letter if property is owner-occupied)
    - 2 years of T776 Rental Income schedules if property has been owned for 2+ years
    - Most recent NOA

    **Notes for Realtors and Financial Planners:**
    Clients with multiple rental properties can see their TDS ratios managed effectively when rental income is structured correctly. The key is matching the client to the lender with the most favourable rental offset policy. See the Rental Portfolio Structuring guide for a full breakdown.
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Pension / CPP / OAS">
    **How Lenders Calculate It**

    Pension income, Canada Pension Plan (CPP) benefits, and Old Age Security (OAS) are among the most stable and lender-friendly income types. Lenders accept these at 100% of their stated value, and in many cases apply a gross-up for tax-advantaged amounts.

    **What qualifies:**
    - Employer pension (defined benefit or defined contribution drawdown)
    - CPP retirement benefits
    - OAS benefits
    - RRSP/RRIF drawdowns (treated as income if consistent; documentation of sustainability may be required)
    - Foreign pension income (subject to currency conversion and stability verification)

    **Gross-up eligibility:**
    - CPP disability and certain pension income types may qualify for a 25% gross-up at many lenders, increasing the qualifying income figure

    **Documentation required:**
    - Pension award letter or pension statement
    - CPP/OAS benefit confirmation from Service Canada
    - Most recent NOA
    - Bank statements showing consistent deposit history

    **Notes for Realtors and Financial Planners:**
    Retired clients are often stronger mortgage candidates than they expect, particularly when pension income is stable and other debts are minimal. The key variables are TDS management and down payment size, not income instability.
  </Tab>

  <Tab title="Investment / Dividend Income">
    **How Lenders Calculate It**

    Investment income — including dividends, interest income, and capital gains — is treated conservatively by most lenders. The core concern is sustainability: lenders want to see that the income is recurring, documented over multiple years, and likely to continue.

    **Calculation method:**
    - 2-year average of investment income as reported on the T1 General
    - Some lenders require the income to be documented for 2 years **and** expected to continue for at least 3 years going forward
    - Capital gains are generally excluded or heavily discounted — they are non-recurring by nature
    - Dividend income from a private corporation held by the borrower is subject to additional scrutiny (lenders look through to the underlying corporation's health)

    **Documentation required:**
    - 2 years of T1 Generals showing investment income on Schedule 4
    - 2 years of NOAs
    - Investment account statements confirming holdings and income

    **Notes for Realtors and Financial Planners:**
    Clients who live primarily on investment income should be referred early — this profile requires careful lender matching. Some lenders are significantly more accommodating of investment income than others. Asset-based lending programs at certain institutions may offer an alternative qualification pathway for high-net-worth clients with strong asset portfolios but low reported income.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Warning>
  **Declining Income:** If a client's income in the most recent tax year is **lower** than the prior year — regardless of income type — most lenders will use the **lower figure** rather than the 2-year average. This is particularly impactful for commission earners, self-employed borrowers, and anyone who took a career break. A client who earned $120,000 two years ago but $85,000 last year qualifies on $85,000. Flag declining income patterns early so we can assess lender options and structuring alternatives before the client has an accepted offer.
</Warning>

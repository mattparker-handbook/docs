Self-employed mortgage applications require specialist handling — full stop. The income that appears on a self-employed borrower's personal tax return is often a poor reflection of their actual financial strength, and lenders know it. The challenge is proving that strength in a format lenders accept. When a self-employed client is referred to us early — before they've started their property search — we can identify the qualifying income, align expectations, and in many cases improve the outcome meaningfully before an application is submitted.

## Two Pathways for Self-Employed Borrowers

Not all self-employed mortgages are created equal. The pathway a borrower takes depends on how their income is structured, how it is documented, and how it is reported to CRA. Each pathway has different rates, lender access, and documentation requirements.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Traditional (Fully Disclosed)" icon="file-check">
    **Best for:** Business owners with 2+ years of filed returns showing sufficient net income to qualify

    The borrower's qualifying income is calculated from their T1 General and NOA over the past 2 years. This is the same standard applied to employed borrowers, using personal income as declared to CRA. When the numbers work, this pathway delivers access to A lenders — major banks and monolines — at the best available rates. CMHC-insured products are available with as little as 5–10% down for qualifying files.

    The tradeoff is that income must be declared. Borrowers who have aggressively minimized taxable income for tax purposes may find their reported net income is insufficient to qualify at the purchase price they want.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Stated Income / Alt-A" icon="building-columns">
    **Best for:** Business owners with strong cash flow but low declared income, or those who haven't been self-employed for 2 full years

    The borrower states a gross income that is supported by business evidence — bank statements, revenue figures, and an accountant's letter — rather than CRA-filed net income. This pathway routes through B lenders and alternative lenders, which means higher rates (typically 1–3% above A lender rates), a minimum 20% down payment, and lender fees.

    This is not a last resort — it is the appropriate channel for a specific type of borrower. The rate premium is often worth it to a client who can purchase now, build equity, and refinance into an A lender at renewal once 2 years of stronger declared income have been filed.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Key Documentation Required

The documentation requirements for self-employed borrowers are more extensive than for T4-employed clients. Gathering these documents early prevents delays and demonstrates to lenders that the file is organized and credible.

| Document | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| T1 General — 2 years | Confirms personal net income declared to CRA | Line 15000 is the primary qualifying income figure |
| Notice of Assessment (NOA) — 2 years | Confirms the T1 was filed and accepted by CRA | Must match the T1; any balance owing should be addressed |
| Business registration or articles of incorporation | Confirms the business legally exists | NUANS report or provincial registry confirmation accepted |
| 6-month business bank statements | Shows actual business cash flow and revenue | Required for stated income / Alt-A pathway; helpful for all files |
| Accountant's letter | Confirms ongoing business operation and owner's role | Preferred by most A lenders; required by some |
| Corporate T2 returns — 2 years | Confirms corporate income, retained earnings, and financial health | Required by many A lenders for incorporated borrowers |
| Financial statements — 2 years | Provides detailed picture of business revenue and expenses | Prepared by accountant; adds credibility to stated income files |

## Income Optimization Strategies

For Financial Planners in particular, the following strategies can materially improve a self-employed client's mortgage qualification — but they require advance planning, ideally in the tax years before a purchase is contemplated.

<Accordion title="Adding Back CCA (Capital Cost Allowance)">
  Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) is the tax depreciation a business takes on eligible assets. It is a non-cash deduction — it reduces reported income without reducing actual cash flow. Some A lenders and most Alt lenders will add back CCA to the borrower's declared income for the purpose of calculating qualifying income.

  Example: A client's T1 shows net business income of $80,000 after CCA of $30,000. With a lender that permits CCA add-back, qualifying income becomes $110,000. This can be the difference between qualifying and not qualifying at the target purchase price.

  Not all lenders accept this add-back, and the asset must be documented. Work with us and the client's accountant to confirm which lenders will accept the add-back and whether the specific CCA claimed is eligible.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Using Retained Corporate Earnings as an Income Supplement">
  For incorporated business owners, retained earnings held inside the corporation can sometimes be used as a qualifying factor — particularly in alternative lending scenarios or for specific bank programs designed for high-net-worth borrowers. The lender evaluates whether the retained earnings represent accessible, stable wealth that supports the borrower's ability to service the mortgage.

  This is not a universal lender policy, but it is an important tool for clients who have been building corporate wealth while drawing minimal personal income. The strength of the corporate balance sheet can sometimes compensate for modest personal declared income.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="FHSA and RRSP Strategies for Self-Employed Owners">
  Self-employed business owners are eligible for both the First Home Savings Account (FHSA) and the Home Buyers' Plan (HBP) under the RRSP program, and can use both in combination for a first home purchase.

  - **FHSA:** Up to $8,000/year in contributions (lifetime max $40,000), fully deductible, and tax-free on withdrawal for a qualifying first home purchase. For self-employed clients, FHSA contributions in higher-income years are particularly efficient.
  - **HBP:** Up to $35,000 per borrower ($70,000 for two first-time buyers) can be withdrawn from RRSP tax-free for a qualifying home purchase, with repayment over 15 years.

  Financial Planners should ensure these accounts are funded as early as possible — both for the tax deduction benefit and to ensure the withdrawal is available when the purchase happens.
</Accordion>

<Tip>
  The standard requirement for self-employment is **2 full years** of self-employment history before a lender will consider it for income qualification. However, there is an important exception: if a borrower recently transitioned from **T4 employment to self-employment in the same field or industry**, some lenders will treat the self-employment income more favourably — particularly if the transition was recent and the income is well-documented. A trades contractor who was previously an employee and recently incorporated, for example, may qualify sooner than the 2-year rule implies. Contact us before telling a recently self-employed client that they must wait 2 years.
</Tip>

<Warning>
  Aggressive tax write-offs are the single most common reason self-employed borrowers are disappointed when they learn their qualifying mortgage amount. A business owner earning $200,000 in gross revenue who claims $120,000 in expenses has declared net income of $80,000 — and that is the income the lender uses. The tax savings over multiple years may be significant, but so is the mortgage qualification shortfall. This is a planning conversation worth having with clients well in advance of a purchase. We can provide a qualifying income analysis at any time to help clients and their advisors understand the tradeoff.
</Warning>

Once your mortgage is in place, new questions arise over time — about missed payments, insurance, taxes, what happens when you sell, and the legal side of homeownership in BC. The answers below address the questions BC homeowners ask most often during the life of their mortgage.

<Accordion title="What happens if I miss a mortgage payment?">
  If you miss a mortgage payment, your lender will typically attempt to contact you within the first 15–30 days, and most will apply a late payment fee during that period. A missed payment is generally reported to the credit bureaus (Equifax and TransUnion) once it is **30 or more days past due**, at which point your credit score can be meaningfully affected. If payments remain outstanding past **90 days**, the lender will typically escalate the file, issue a formal notice of default, and begin reviewing legal enforcement options — in BC, this means court-supervised foreclosure proceedings. If you're experiencing financial difficulty, **contact your lender proactively before missing a payment** — most lenders have hardship programs, payment deferrals, and amortization extensions available if you reach out early, and a lender-approved relief measure typically will not be reported to the bureaus.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What is mortgage default insurance and do I have to pay it?">
  Mortgage default insurance (commonly called CMHC insurance, after the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation) is required on any home purchase in Canada where the down payment is less than 20% of the purchase price. It protects the lender — not you — in the event of default. The premium ranges from 0.60% to 4.00% of the mortgage amount, depending on the size of your down payment, and is typically added to your mortgage balance. If you put 20% or more down, default insurance is not required. Once you have a high-ratio insured mortgage, the insurance remains in place for the life of the mortgage — there is no process to "remove" it mid-term by reaching 20% equity.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How do I remove CMHC mortgage insurance from my mortgage?">
  You cannot remove CMHC (or Sagen/Canada Guaranty) default insurance from your mortgage once it has been applied — the premium is a sunk cost that was paid (or added to your balance) at the time of purchase. The insurance remains attached to the mortgage for its full life. The only way to remove it is to pay off and discharge the mortgage entirely, or to refinance into a new conventional mortgage once your equity reaches at least 20% of your home's current appraised value. In the latter case, the new mortgage would be uninsured and would not carry a default insurance premium, but standard refinance costs and re-qualification apply.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Can I add someone to my mortgage after closing?">
  Yes, but it's not a simple administrative change. Adding a person to your mortgage after closing requires a formal application to your lender, full re-qualification with the new borrower included, and a legal title transfer registered by a notary or lawyer. The lender will assess the new borrower's income, credit, and debt profile alongside yours. If you are adding a partner or family member for estate planning purposes, there may also be property transfer tax implications in BC — consult a lawyer or notary before proceeding. In some cases, it may be more efficient to wait until renewal to make this change.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What does 'porting' a mortgage mean?">
  Porting allows you to transfer your existing mortgage — including your current rate and terms — to a new property when you move. This is valuable when your existing rate is lower than current market rates, as it lets you carry that rate to your next home. To port, you must qualify for the mortgage on the new property under current lending rules, and the transfer must typically occur within a specific window (often 30–90 days). If the new property costs more than your current mortgage balance, you will need to top up with additional financing, which is blended with your existing rate. Not all mortgage products are portable — check your commitment letter before listing your current home.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What is a bridge loan and when would I need one?">
  A bridge loan is a short-term, high-rate loan used to cover the gap between the closing date of your new home purchase and the closing date of your current home sale. For example, if your new home closes on June 1 and your existing home doesn't close until June 15, you need funds to complete the purchase before your sale proceeds arrive. Your lender or broker can arrange a bridge loan to fund that gap, typically at prime plus 2–4%. Bridge loans are normally repaid automatically when your sale closes. Most major banks and lenders offer bridge financing, but you typically need a firm sale agreement in place to qualify.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How are my property taxes handled through my mortgage?">
  Some lenders require you to remit property taxes through your mortgage payment — they collect a portion with each payment and pay your municipality directly when taxes are due. Other lenders allow you to pay your property taxes directly to your municipality. In BC, homeowners can also access the **Home Owner Grant** to reduce property taxes on a primary residence. If your lender is collecting taxes, confirm that they are actually remitting payment on time, as you remain ultimately responsible for any late payment penalties. If you prefer to manage your own taxes, ask your lender whether that option is available on your product.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What happens to my home if I default on my mortgage in BC?">
  In BC, the legal process a lender uses to recover a property after mortgage default is **court-supervised foreclosure** — not power of sale, which is the process used in Ontario and several other provinces. If a borrower misses multiple payments and does not resolve the arrears, the lender petitions the BC Supreme Court for an Order Nisi, which establishes a redemption period — typically six months — during which the borrower can still pay out the arrears and reinstate the mortgage. If the borrower cannot redeem within that period, the court issues an Order for Conduct of Sale and the property is sold through a court-approved process. Because the entire process runs through the courts, it is more borrower-protective than power of sale, but it also means the timeline from first missed payment to completed sale can run nine to eighteen months. Lenders strongly prefer to resolve defaults without going to court — if you are in arrears, contact your lender or a housing counsellor as early as possible.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Can my lender call my mortgage due?">
  Under a standard closed mortgage in Canada, your lender cannot demand full repayment before your maturity date unless you have breached the mortgage terms. Common trigger events that could allow a lender to accelerate repayment include: sustained non-payment, failing to maintain adequate home insurance, failing to pay property taxes, misrepresentation on your application, or making unauthorized changes to the property. These circumstances are uncommon, but reviewing your mortgage commitment and maintaining your obligations — insurance, tax payments, regular payments — ensures your lender cannot exercise an acceleration clause against you.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What is title insurance and is it required?">
  Title insurance is a policy that protects you (and your lender) against losses arising from title defects, encroachments, survey issues, title fraud, and zoning violations — matters that may not appear on a standard title search. In BC, most lenders require title insurance as a condition of mortgage funding. It is typically obtained through your notary or lawyer at closing, and a one-time premium is paid — there are no ongoing costs. The coverage lasts as long as you own the property (owner's policy) or as long as the mortgage is outstanding (lender's policy). It is strongly recommended regardless of whether it is required.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How does my mortgage affect my credit score?">
  Your mortgage is reported to the credit bureaus and has a significant influence on your overall credit profile. Making all payments on time builds a strong credit history and demonstrates your ability to manage large installment debt responsibly — both of which improve your credit score over time. Missing payments, being placed in arrears, or having your mortgage go to collections will severely damage your credit and remain on your bureau for up to six years. Your mortgage also affects your credit utilization ratio and overall debt load, which lenders consider when you apply for any new credit in the future.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What happens to my mortgage when I sell my home?">
  When you sell your home, the outstanding mortgage balance is repaid at closing from your sale proceeds — this is handled automatically by your notary or lawyer as part of the transaction. If you have an open mortgage or are selling at your renewal date, there is no prepayment penalty. If your mortgage is closed and mid-term, a prepayment penalty will apply — request a penalty quote from your lender as soon as you begin considering a sale. Alternatively, if you are buying another property, you may be able to port your mortgage to avoid the penalty. Your broker can help you understand all options before you list.
</Accordion>

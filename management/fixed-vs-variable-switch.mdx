Most borrowers choose between fixed and variable rates at the time of purchase or renewal — but occasionally, circumstances change mid-term and you find yourself wondering whether it's worth making a switch before your maturity date. Whether you're on a variable rate and want the security of a fixed payment, or you're on a fixed rate and want to take advantage of a rate drop, the rules and costs are very different depending on which direction you're moving.

## Converting Variable to Fixed Mid-Term

Switching from a variable rate to a fixed rate is the simpler of the two conversions, and most lenders allow it without charging a prepayment penalty.

**Key things to understand:**

- **One-directional flexibility:** Most lender mortgage contracts permit you to convert variable to fixed at any time during the term, without penalty. The reverse — fixed to variable — is not typically permitted without breaking the mortgage.
- **The new fixed rate is based on current market rates** — not your existing variable rate or the rate you started with. If fixed rates have risen since you took out your variable mortgage, you may be locking in at a higher rate than you expected.
- **Your term typically resets.** When you convert, most lenders require you to take a new term of at least 3–5 years. This may extend your overall commitment significantly — factor that into your decision.
- **Your amortization does not reset** — the remaining amortization continues from where you left off.

<Note>
  Many borrowers instinctively want to lock in when they feel anxious about rising rates — but that anxiety often peaks near the top of a rate cycle, precisely when fixed rates are at their highest. Converting variable to fixed at the wrong time can mean you lock in at an elevated rate just before variable rates stabilize or decline. Think carefully about your timing before converting.
</Note>

## Converting Fixed to Variable Mid-Term

Moving from a fixed rate to a variable rate mid-term is significantly more complex and expensive, because it requires breaking your existing fixed mortgage.

**What this involves:**

- **Prepayment penalty:** Breaking a fixed mortgage triggers either an IRD (Interest Rate Differential) penalty or a three-month interest penalty — whichever is greater. IRD penalties on fixed mortgages can easily reach $10,000–$25,000 depending on your lender, rate, and remaining term.
- **When it may make sense:** If market rates have dropped substantially and you have a long remaining term, the ongoing monthly savings from a lower variable rate can eventually offset the penalty. The break-even calculation tells you whether the math works.
- **New mortgage costs:** You'll also incur legal fees ($1,000–$1,500) and potentially an appraisal fee, unless you stay with the same lender on a conversion product.

## Break-Even Calculation Example

Use this framework to decide whether switching from fixed to variable is financially worthwhile:

**Scenario:**
- Outstanding mortgage balance: $500,000
- Current fixed rate: 5.75%
- New variable rate available: 4.75%
- Monthly payment difference (savings): ~$300/month
- IRD prepayment penalty: $8,000
- Legal and other fees: ~$1,500
- **Total cost to switch: $9,500**

**Break-even calculation:**
$$\frac{\$9,500}{\$300/\text{month}} = \approx 32 \text{ months}$$

If you keep the mortgage for more than 32 months after switching, you come out ahead. If your remaining term is shorter than that — or you plan to sell or refinance again soon — the switch likely doesn't make financial sense.

<Tip>
  In most cases, the smartest move is to **wait until renewal** before changing your rate type. At renewal, you can move from fixed to variable (or vice versa) with no penalty, no legal fees, and no break-even calculation required. Unless the interest savings are very large and your remaining term is long, patience is usually the better strategy.
</Tip>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<Accordion title="Can I switch lenders to get a better rate mid-term?">
  Yes, but switching lenders mid-term means breaking your current mortgage, which triggers a prepayment penalty. The same break-even logic applies — you need the savings from the new rate to outweigh the penalty and switching costs over your remaining term. If your penalty is large (as is common with fixed-rate mortgages), it is often more cost-effective to wait until renewal. Your broker can model both scenarios for you.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="If I convert my variable rate to fixed, does my amortization reset?">
  No. Converting from variable to fixed does not reset your amortization. Your remaining amortization continues from where it stands at the time of conversion. What does change is your term — most lenders require you to take a new minimum term of 3–5 years when you convert. Your payment amount will be recalculated based on your current balance, remaining amortization, and the new fixed rate.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Does converting variable to fixed cost anything?">
  In most cases, no — converting variable to fixed mid-term does not trigger a prepayment penalty. However, you should confirm this with your specific lender before proceeding, as mortgage contracts vary. There may be minor administrative fees in some cases, but they are typically minimal compared to the cost of breaking a fixed mortgage.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What if I'm on a variable rate and my lender increases the prime rate again?">
  If you're on a variable rate and rates rise, you have the option to convert to fixed at any time without penalty — but the new fixed rate will reflect current market conditions, which may also be elevated. Before locking in out of concern about future rate increases, consider how much room there is for further rate movement and whether the fixed rate available today is genuinely better than what you expect over your remaining term. Consult your broker before making a reactive decision.
</Accordion>

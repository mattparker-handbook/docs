Your mortgage renewal is one of the most important financial decisions you'll make as a homeowner. Unlike your original purchase, renewal doesn't come with the same sense of urgency or excitement — which means it's easy to simply sign whatever your lender sends and move on. But the choices you make at renewal directly affect the interest rate you'll pay, the flexibility you'll have, and how quickly you'll build equity over your next term. Taking the time to shop around and plan ahead can save you tens of thousands of dollars.

## When to Start Your Renewal Process

The ideal time to begin your renewal planning is **120 days (four months) before your maturity date**. Most lenders will send you a renewal offer around this time, and many will honour an early rate hold so you can lock in a competitive rate before conditions change.

Starting early gives you time to:
- Compare offers from multiple lenders and mortgage brokers
- Request a penalty quote if you're considering switching lenders
- Complete any required documentation without the pressure of a looming deadline
- Negotiate better terms rather than accepting the first offer

<Tip>
  Mark your maturity date in your calendar the moment you sign your mortgage. Set a reminder for 120 days prior so renewal planning doesn't sneak up on you.
</Tip>

## The Renewal Process Step by Step

<Steps>
  <Step title="Review Your Current Mortgage Details">
    Pull out your current mortgage documents and confirm your maturity date, outstanding balance, current rate, amortization remaining, and any prepayment privileges you've used. This is your baseline for evaluating whether to stay or switch.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Engage a Mortgage Broker (120+ Days Out)">
    Contact your mortgage broker well before renewal. A broker can access rates from dozens of lenders simultaneously and identify whether your current lender's offer is competitive. This step costs you nothing and gives you significant negotiating leverage.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Evaluate Your Lender's Renewal Offer">
    When your lender sends a renewal offer, don't sign it immediately. Review the rate, term options, prepayment privileges, and any restrictions. Compare it directly against what your broker has sourced from competing lenders.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Consider Switching If Better Terms Are Available">
    If another lender is offering a meaningfully better rate or more favourable terms, calculate the full cost of switching — including legal fees and any appraisal requirements. Even after those costs, switching is often the better financial decision.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Submit Your Renewal or Switch Application">
    Once you've made a decision, complete the required paperwork. Renewing with your current lender is straightforward. Switching requires a new application, income verification, and a property appraisal in some cases — start this process early to ensure completion before your maturity date.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Sign Your New Mortgage Documents">
    Review your new mortgage commitment carefully before signing. Confirm the rate, term, payment amount, prepayment privileges, and any conditions. If switching lenders, your notary or lawyer will handle the discharge and registration of the new mortgage.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Should You Stay or Switch?

The answer depends on your specific situation — but the default instinct to simply renew with your current lender often costs more than it should. The table below summarizes the key trade-offs.

| Factor | Stay with Current Lender | Switch to New Lender |
|---|---|---|
| Convenience | High | Lower (paperwork required) |
| Rate | Often higher (renewal penalty risk) | Often lower |
| Cost to switch | $0 | Legal fees (~$1,000–$1,500) |
| Appraisal | Usually not required | May be required |
| Opportunity | Refinance not possible | Can refinance and pull equity |

In general, staying with your current lender makes sense when the rate difference is small (under 0.15%), your remaining amortization is short, or you have no need to access equity. Switching is worth the effort when the rate gap is meaningful, you want to consolidate debt or access equity, or your current lender's terms are more restrictive than alternatives in the market.

<Tip>
  Never sign your lender's first renewal offer without shopping it around first. Lenders routinely send renewal letters at posted rates — well above what's available to a qualified borrower who takes the time to ask. A five-minute conversation with a broker can easily save you thousands of dollars.
</Tip>

<Warning>
  **Automatic renewal risk:** If you do nothing before your maturity date, most lenders will automatically renew your mortgage — typically at a posted or "default" rate, which is among the highest rates available. This can cost you significantly more than a negotiated or brokered renewal. Always respond to your renewal offer before the deadline.
</Warning>

## What to Negotiate at Renewal

Even if you plan to stay with your current lender, most terms are negotiable at renewal. Consider asking about:

- **Rate reduction** — use competing offers as leverage
- **Amortization extension** — can lower your payment if cash flow is tight
- **Prepayment privileges** — ensure you have the flexibility you need (10–20% lump sum, payment increase)
- **Portability** — important if you may move before your next renewal
- **Shorter term** — if you expect rates to drop, a 1–2 year term may outperform a 5-year lock-in

The renewal conversation is also a good time to reassess your overall financial picture. If your income has increased, your home has appreciated, or your goals have changed, renewal is the moment to recalibrate your mortgage structure accordingly.

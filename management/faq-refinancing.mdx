Refinancing is one of the most powerful tools available to Canadian homeowners, but it also raises more questions than almost any other mortgage topic. The answers below address the most common questions about when refinancing makes sense, what it costs, and how the process works in BC.

<Accordion title="What is the difference between renewal and refinancing?">
  Renewal is the automatic process of continuing your mortgage at the end of a term — typically with the same lender and balance, just at new terms and a new rate. Refinancing is a deliberate restructuring of your mortgage: you change the amount, terms, lender, or all three. Refinancing can happen at renewal (with no penalty) or mid-term (with a prepayment penalty). The key difference is that refinancing involves re-qualifying with a lender, and it often changes the outstanding balance — either to access equity or to consolidate other debts.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How much of my home equity can I access through refinancing?">
  Under OSFI guidelines, the maximum you can refinance your mortgage to is **80% of your home's appraised value**. For example, if your home is worth $800,000, the maximum new mortgage is $640,000. If your current mortgage balance is $450,000, you could access up to $190,000 in equity through a refinance. Keep in mind that the value used is based on a lender-ordered appraisal at the time of the refinance — not your estimate or a previous purchase price. If your home has appreciated significantly, you may have more available equity than you realize.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Can I refinance mid-term or only at renewal?">
  You can refinance at any point during your mortgage term, but refinancing mid-term means breaking your existing mortgage, which triggers a prepayment penalty. For fixed-rate mortgages, the penalty is typically the greater of three months' interest or the Interest Rate Differential (IRD), which can be substantial. For variable-rate mortgages, the penalty is typically three months' interest, which is more predictable. Many borrowers choose to wait until their renewal date to avoid the penalty entirely, particularly if their maturity date is within 12 months.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What costs should I expect when refinancing?">
  The main costs involved in a refinance are: the prepayment penalty (if breaking mid-term), legal or notary fees ($1,000–$2,000), a property appraisal ($300–$600), title insurance ($200–$400), and any lender or brokerage fees ($0–$1,000 depending on the product). The prepayment penalty is typically the largest variable and can range from a few hundred dollars on a variable mortgage to $25,000 or more on a large fixed-rate mortgage with significant rate differential. Always get a formal penalty quote in writing from your lender before proceeding.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How long does a refinance take in BC?">
  From application to funding, a typical mortgage refinance in BC takes **3–6 weeks**. The timeline depends on how quickly you can provide documentation, how long the lender takes to approve the application, the availability of the appraiser, and scheduling with a notary or lawyer to complete the legal work. If you are under a tight deadline — for example, to fund a renovation or purchase — notify your broker upfront so timelines can be managed accordingly. Complex files (self-employed income, unique properties) may take longer.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Can I consolidate debt into my mortgage through a refinance?">
  Yes. Debt consolidation is one of the most common reasons homeowners refinance. By rolling high-interest debt (credit cards, lines of credit, personal loans, car loans) into your mortgage, you convert that debt to mortgage-rate interest — typically far lower than what you were paying. For example, paying 24% on a $30,000 credit card balance versus 5.5% on the same amount included in your mortgage results in thousands of dollars in annual savings. The key is to ensure you have enough equity in your home (up to the 80% LTV limit) and that you don't accumulate new consumer debt after the consolidation.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Will refinancing hurt my credit score?">
  Refinancing involves a credit inquiry, which will temporarily reduce your credit score by a small amount (typically 5–10 points). This impact is short-lived. Paying off credit card balances or other consumer debts through a consolidation refinance can actually improve your credit utilization ratio and benefit your overall credit profile over time. If you are working on improving your credit for another purpose (a car loan, business financing), you may want to time your refinance accordingly, but the credit impact of refinancing alone is rarely a reason to delay.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Can I refinance if my home value has decreased?">
  If your home's appraised value has declined, your available equity is reduced — and refinancing may not be possible if your current mortgage balance is already close to or above 80% LTV. In cases where equity is insufficient, alternatives include a second mortgage through a private lender (at higher rates), waiting until values recover, or making additional principal payments to build equity before refinancing. Your broker can assess your specific situation and advise on what options are realistically available.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How do I know if refinancing will save me money?">
  Start with a **break-even analysis**: add up all the costs of refinancing (penalty, legal, appraisal, etc.) and divide that total by the monthly savings you'll achieve from the new rate or restructured payments. The result tells you how many months it takes to recover your costs. If you plan to keep the property and mortgage beyond that break-even point, refinancing saves money overall. Also consider the non-interest benefits — improved cash flow, debt consolidation relief, or access to equity for a high-return investment — which may justify the cost even when the rate savings alone don't.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Can I refinance a rental property?">
  Yes, rental properties can be refinanced in BC. The process is similar to refinancing a primary residence, but qualifying criteria differ: lenders typically apply a rental income offset (often 50–80% of gross rental income is added to your qualifying income), and interest rates on investment properties are usually 0.10–0.25% higher than on owner-occupied properties. The maximum LTV for a refinance remains 80%. Lenders will also review the property's condition through an appraisal. If the rental is in a limited-access location or has rental income from informal arrangements, qualification may be more complex — speak with a broker who works regularly with investment property files.
</Accordion>

Prepayment penalties are one of the most financially significant — and most underestimated — features of a Canadian mortgage. When you break your mortgage before the end of the term, your lender is entitled to compensation for the interest income they lose as a result. The method used to calculate that compensation depends on your lender type, your rate type, and how much time remains in your term. Penalties can range from a few hundred dollars on a small variable-rate mortgage to $30,000 or more on a large fixed-rate mortgage at a Big Bank. Understanding the mechanics before you select a lender is not optional — it is part of making a financially sound mortgage decision.

## The Two Penalty Methods

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="3-Month Interest">
    **The simpler, more borrower-friendly method.**

    Three months of interest is calculated on your outstanding mortgage balance at your contract interest rate. This method is standard for **variable-rate mortgages** across most lenders, and is also used by some credit unions and a small number of monoline lenders for fixed-rate products.

    **Formula:**
    ```
    Penalty = Outstanding Balance × Annual Interest Rate ÷ 12 × 3
    ```

    **Example:**
    - Outstanding balance: $500,000
    - Contract rate: 5.50%
    - Monthly interest: $500,000 × 5.50% ÷ 12 = $2,292
    - 3-month penalty: $2,292 × 3 = **$6,875**

    This is a predictable, straightforward number. It scales with balance but not with how far rates have moved since origination.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Interest Rate Differential (IRD)">
    **The more complex, often larger method used for fixed-rate mortgages.**

    IRD compensates the lender for the difference between the rate on your mortgage and the rate they could offer today for the remaining term. If rates have fallen since you took your mortgage, the lender is losing income — and IRD quantifies that loss.

    **Formula (simplified):**
    ```
    Penalty = Outstanding Balance × (Your Rate − Comparable Current Rate) × Remaining Term in Years
    ```

    The critical variable is which rate is used as your "starting rate" — your actual contract rate, or the bank's posted rate. See the Warning below.

    IRD is applied to **fixed-rate mortgages** at most federally regulated lenders when the IRD amount exceeds the 3-month interest calculation. The lender always charges whichever is greater.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## How IRD Is Calculated: Step by Step

<Steps>
  <Step title="Determine Your Outstanding Balance">
    Start with your current mortgage balance at the time of payout — not your original principal. This figure can be obtained from your lender's current mortgage statement or by calling for a payout quote.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Identify Your Contract Rate">
    This is the rate you are currently paying. For Big Bank mortgages, note that this may differ from the "posted rate" that existed when you signed — and that distinction matters significantly for the next step.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find Today's Comparable Term Rate">
    Your lender will use the rate they currently offer for a term closest to your remaining term. For example, if you have 3 years and 1 month remaining, they will likely use their current 3-year fixed rate. This is the rate they could lend the money at today.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Calculate the Rate Differential">
    Subtract today's comparable rate from your rate (or posted rate, depending on lender method). This differential, multiplied across your balance and remaining term, produces the IRD.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply the Formula">
    Multiply: `Outstanding Balance × Rate Differential × Remaining Term (in years)`.
  </Step>
</Steps>

**Worked Example:**

| Variable | Value |
|---|---|
| Outstanding balance | $500,000 |
| Your contract rate | 5.50% |
| Today's comparable 3-year rate | 4.20% |
| Rate differential | 1.30% |
| Remaining term | 3 years |
| **Estimated IRD Penalty** | **$500,000 × 1.30% × 3 = $19,500** |

<Warning>
  **Big Banks use posted rates in IRD calculations — and it dramatically increases the penalty.**

  Most Big Banks do not use your actual discounted contract rate in the IRD formula. Instead, they use the **posted rate** that existed when you took your mortgage, and compare it to today's posted rate for the comparable remaining term. Because the spread between posted and discounted rates is typically 1.0–2.0%, this method produces penalties that are materially larger than a contract-rate calculation for the same mortgage.

  **Example of the impact — same $500,000 mortgage, 3 years remaining:**

  | Method | Starting Rate | Today's Comparable Rate | Differential | Penalty |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Monoline (contract rate) | 5.50% | 4.20% | 1.30% | **$19,500** |
  | Big Bank (posted rate) | 7.09% | 5.54% | 1.55% | **$23,250** |

  The posted-rate method results in a ~$3,750 larger penalty on the same balance — and the gap widens with larger balances and longer remaining terms. Always request a formal payout statement from your lender and never estimate a Big Bank IRD using your contract rate alone.
</Warning>

## Bank IRD vs. Monoline IRD

| Method | Used By | Calculation Base | Typical Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank IRD (posted rate differential) | Big Six Banks | Posted rate at origination minus today's posted rate for remaining term | Very large — can be $15,000–$40,000+ |
| Monoline IRD (contract rate differential) | Most monoline lenders | Your actual contract rate minus today's rate for remaining term | Moderate — typically smaller than bank IRD |
| 3-Month Interest | Variable-rate mortgages (all lenders); some CU fixed products | Contract interest rate × outstanding balance × 3 months | Small — generally $3,000–$8,000 on a typical balance |

<Tip>
  **Always request a formal payout statement before making any decision that requires breaking your mortgage.** Whether you are listing your property, considering a refinance, or evaluating an early renewal offer, get the exact penalty figure in writing from your lender before committing to any transaction. Mortgage brokers and real estate lawyers cannot give you this number — only your lender can. The quote is typically valid for 30 days.
</Tip>

## When Penalties Are Waived or Reduced

Not every departure from your mortgage term triggers a full penalty. There are four common scenarios where penalties are eliminated or significantly reduced:

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Mortgage Portability">
    If you are selling your home and purchasing a new one, most lenders allow you to **port** your mortgage — transferring the existing rate and terms to the new property. When portability is successfully executed, the prepayment penalty is typically waived. The window to complete the port (usually 60–120 days) and the requirement to re-qualify on the new purchase are the key conditions. See the [Porting and Assumption](/lenders/porting-and-assumption) page for full details.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Blend-and-Extend">
    If you need additional funds (e.g., for renovations or consolidation), some lenders allow you to add new money to your existing mortgage at a blended rate — without fully breaking and re-registering. The blended rate is a weighted average of your current rate and the new market rate. The penalty is either waived entirely or folded into the new blended rate. This is lender-specific and not universally available.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Maturity — No Penalty">
    The simplest scenario: if you wait until the end of your term, there is no prepayment penalty whatsoever. You are free to renew, switch lenders, pay out the balance, or restructure as you wish. This is why keeping your term horizon in mind at origination — and not locking into a 5-year term if you're likely to move in 3 years — is important.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Lender Promotions and Discretionary Waivers">
    Occasionally, lenders will offer penalty waivers or reductions as part of a retention strategy or promotional campaign. These are discretionary, not guaranteed, and should not be factored into a financial plan. However, if you are approaching a lender about breaking your mortgage, it is always worth asking whether any waiver or reduction programs apply to your situation.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

Canada's Big Six chartered banks — RBC, TD, Scotiabank, BMO, CIBC, and National Bank — collectively hold the majority of Canadian residential mortgage debt. Their advantages are real: a complete banking ecosystem under one roof, nationwide branch networks, readvanceable HELOC products, and the credibility that comes with institutional scale. The trade-off is equally real: their proprietary underwriting can be rigid, their posted-rate IRD penalty calculations can produce shockingly large break costs, and their rates are rarely the sharpest in the market. For the right borrower — particularly one who values an all-in-one financial relationship or needs a readvanceable HELOC — a Big Bank mortgage is a legitimate choice. For a rate-focused borrower with a straightforward qualifying profile, the numbers often tell a different story.

## Advantages of Big Bank Mortgages

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="All-In-One Banking Relationship">
    Big Banks offer the ability to consolidate your mortgage, HELOC, chequing, savings, investments, and credit cards under a single institution. For clients who value simplicity, consolidated reporting, and the convenience of a single point of contact for all financial products, this integration is a meaningful benefit. Products like TD's Home Equity FlexLine and RBC's Homeline Plan are specifically designed to sit alongside your everyday banking.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Branch Network and In-Person Support">
    With hundreds to thousands of physical branches across Canada, Big Banks provide in-person mortgage advice and ongoing support. For clients who are uncomfortable with purely digital or phone-based service — or who require in-branch document handling — this is a genuine differentiator. Rural and smaller-market clients particularly benefit from physical presence.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Wide Product Range">
    Big Banks offer the full spectrum: insured mortgages (high-ratio), conventional mortgages, readvanceable HELOCs, construction loans, commercial mortgages, and bridge financing. They can also accommodate New to Canada programs, foreign income files (to varying degrees), and bundled products that monolines and credit unions typically cannot match.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Brand Recognition and Institutional Stability">
    As Schedule I federally chartered banks, the Big Six operate under OSFI oversight and deposit insurance through CDIC. Their long-term stability is not a meaningful concern for borrowers. For some clients — particularly newcomers to Canada or those who place high value on brand trust — this matters in the decision.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Common Big Bank Policy Traits

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="IRD Prepayment Penalties Using Posted Rates">
    When you break a fixed-rate mortgage at a Big Bank before maturity, the penalty is calculated using the **Interest Rate Differential (IRD)** method. Critically, most Big Banks calculate IRD using their **posted rate** (not your discounted contract rate) as the starting point, and compare it against the posted rate for the nearest remaining term. Because the spread between posted and discounted rates is typically 1–2%, this produces penalties that are materially larger than the same calculation performed using contract rates — which is how most monoline lenders operate. See the [Prepayment Penalties](/lenders/prepayment-penalties) page for a detailed explanation and examples.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Readvanceable HELOC Products">
    All six major banks offer readvanceable mortgage-HELOC combinations, which allow the HELOC component to automatically grow as you pay down your mortgage principal. This makes them the default choice for clients pursuing the Smith Manoeuvre or those who want a long-term revolving credit facility tied to their home equity. Each bank has a proprietary product name for this structure (see table below).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Posted Rate vs. Contract Rate — IRD Implications">
    The distinction between a bank's **posted rate** and your actual **discounted contract rate** is not merely cosmetic. When banks calculate IRD penalties, many use the posted rate as the comparator, which significantly inflates the penalty. For example, if you received a 1.5% discount off posted at origination, that discount is effectively excluded from the penalty calculation — meaning you're penalized as if you borrowed at a higher rate than you actually did. This is a well-documented borrower concern and a key policy difference compared to monoline lenders.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Proprietary Underwriting Standards">
    Each Big Bank uses its own internal underwriting guidelines, which can differ from published OSFI norms. Self-employed borrowers, those with non-traditional income, or applicants with complex financial profiles may find Big Bank underwriting rigid. Unlike alternative lenders or some credit unions, banks generally require T4-verifiable income or two years of NOAs for self-employed clients, and apply conservative rental income offsets.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

<Warning>
  **Big Bank IRD penalties can be extremely large — calculate before you commit.** A borrower with a $700,000 mortgage at a 5-year fixed rate who needs to break their mortgage at year 3 could face a penalty of $20,000–$40,000 or more depending on how far rates have moved and how the bank calculates IRD. Never assume the penalty will be modest. Request a formal penalty calculation directly from your lender before listing your property, refinancing, or making any decision that would require breaking the mortgage.
</Warning>

<Tip>
  **Negotiate your discount at origination to reduce future IRD exposure.** At some Big Banks, the IRD penalty is calculated based on the difference between the posted rate at the time you took your mortgage and the current posted rate for the comparable remaining term. A larger discount off posted at origination can, in some bank formulas, actually *reduce* your IRD penalty if you break early — because your effective rate is lower relative to posted. Ask your broker or bank mortgage specialist to walk through the penalty formula *before* you sign, not after.
</Tip>

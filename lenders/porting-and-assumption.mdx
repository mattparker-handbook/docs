Mortgage portability and mortgage assumption are two features that receive far less attention than they deserve — and in the right circumstances, either one can save a borrower tens of thousands of dollars. Portability allows you to take your existing mortgage rate with you when you move, avoiding a penalty and preserving a rate that may no longer be available in the current market. Assumption allows a buyer to step into the seller's mortgage, effectively inheriting a below-market rate that the seller locked in during a prior rate environment. Both features require lender approval and borrower re-qualification, but for clients in the right situation, the financial case for exploring them is compelling.

## Mortgage Porting

### What Porting Means

Porting is the transfer of your existing mortgage — including the rate, terms, and remaining balance — from your current property to a new property when you move. Rather than breaking your mortgage (and paying a penalty), you carry the existing product forward. The mortgage is effectively discharged on the old property and re-registered on the new one.

### How the Process Works

<Steps>
  <Step title="Confirm Portability with Your Lender">
    Not all mortgages are portable, and not all ports are approved automatically. Confirm with your lender or broker that your mortgage has portability provisions in its terms before listing your property. Most Big Bank and monoline mortgages are portable — but the terms and timelines matter.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply to Port When Buying the New Property">
    When you have a firm purchase agreement on the new property, contact your lender to initiate the port application. You will need to re-qualify on the new purchase — including providing updated income documentation, credit confirmation, and an appraisal on the new property. Portability is not automatic; it is subject to full underwriting approval.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Meet the Timing Window">
    Most lenders require that the sale of the old property and the purchase of the new property close within a defined window — typically **60 to 120 days**. If the closing gap exceeds this window, the port may not be permitted and you may face a penalty on the discharged mortgage. Back-to-back closings on the same day, or a bridge loan to bridge the gap, are common solutions.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Handle the Top-Up (If the New Property Costs More)">
    If your new purchase price is higher than the balance of your existing mortgage, you have two options: (1) a **blend-and-extend**, where the existing balance stays at your current rate and the additional funds are advanced at the current market rate, blended into a single new rate; or (2) a **separate new mortgage** at current market rates for the additional amount, alongside the ported original balance. Each approach has different rate and term implications — model both scenarios.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Handle the Shortfall (If the New Property Costs Less)">
    If you are downsizing and the new property value is lower than your outstanding mortgage balance, you will need to pay down the excess — and that excess portion may be subject to a prepayment penalty. Structure the transaction carefully with your broker to minimize the exposed penalty amount.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Porting Rules by Lender Type

| Lender Type | Portable? | Typical Timeframe | Blend-and-Extend Available? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big Banks | Yes (all six) | 60–120 days | Yes |
| Monoline Lenders | Usually yes | 60–90 days | Sometimes |
| BC Credit Unions | Varies by institution | Varies | Varies |

<Note>
  Even when a mortgage is technically portable, lenders retain the right to decline a port if the new property does not meet their security requirements — for example, if the new property is rural, strata, or has features that the lender's guidelines exclude. Confirm property eligibility at the same time as confirming portability.
</Note>

## Mortgage Assumption

### What Assumption Means

Mortgage assumption is the process by which a **buyer takes over the seller's existing mortgage** — including the outstanding balance, interest rate, remaining term, and all associated terms. The buyer steps into the seller's position on the mortgage, and the seller is released from liability (subject to lender approval). Unlike porting, assumption is initiated by the buyer, not the seller.

### Requirements for Assumption

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Buyer Must Qualify">
    The assuming buyer must meet the lender's qualification standards — income, credit score, and debt service ratios — as if they were applying for a new mortgage. The lender is not obligated to approve the assumption simply because the seller wants to sell. This is a full underwriting process.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Lender Must Approve">
    All assumptions require explicit lender consent. The lender reviews the buyer's financial profile, the property value (which may trigger a new appraisal), and the terms of the transaction. If the lender declines the assumption, the sale either proceeds with conventional financing or falls apart.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Seller's Release from Liability">
    The seller should obtain a formal release of liability from the lender upon assumption completion. Without this release, the seller may remain on the hook if the assuming buyer subsequently defaults — even though they no longer own the property. This is a critical step that is sometimes overlooked in the transaction.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

### Why Assumption Is Attractive

In a rising or elevated rate environment, a seller who locked in a 5-year fixed rate at 2.0% or 2.5% in 2021 holds a significant asset in that rate. A buyer who can assume that mortgage at well below current market rates is saving real money every month for the remaining term — and that savings can be reflected in the purchase price negotiation, making the deal more attractive to both parties.

**Example scenario:** Seller has $450,000 remaining on a mortgage at 2.49% with 2.5 years remaining. Buyer is facing today's rate of 4.75%. On $450,000, the monthly payment difference is approximately $525/month — or roughly $15,750 in interest savings over the assumed term. That is a meaningful number in any price negotiation.

<Note>
  **Assumable mortgages are relatively uncommon in today's market** — most lenders include "due on sale" clauses that require full discharge upon property transfer. Truly assumable mortgages are typically CMHC-insured mortgages originated when assumption was standard practice, or specific lender products where assumability is an explicit feature. Always verify assumability through the mortgage documents or directly with the lender — do not rely on listing descriptions or verbal representations.
</Note>

<Tip>
  **For sellers with low-rate fixed mortgages, assumability can be a genuine marketing advantage.** In a high-rate environment, a property with an assumable mortgage at a substantially below-market rate is objectively more attractive to a qualified buyer. Listing agents and sellers should understand the rate, balance, remaining term, and lender policy on assumption before deciding how to present this feature in the marketing of the property. A mortgage broker can help calculate the value of the assumption to a prospective buyer.
</Tip>

Monoline lenders are federally regulated financial institutions that do one thing exclusively: mortgages. They have no branches, no chequing accounts, no credit cards, and no walk-in customers. They operate entirely through the broker channel, which means they are invisible to borrowers who go directly to a bank — and disproportionately beneficial to those who don't. Funded through institutional capital markets and CMHC securitization programs, monolines pass their structural cost savings to borrowers in the form of lower rates, more transparent penalty calculations, and leaner mortgage products purpose-built for the borrower rather than the bank's cross-sell agenda.

## Why Monolines Often Have Better Rates

<Steps>
  <Step title="No Branch Overhead">
    Monolines carry none of the fixed costs associated with maintaining a national branch network — no retail locations, no tellers, no branch managers. This structural efficiency translates directly into margin savings that can be passed to borrowers in the form of lower rates.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Institutional Funding via Capital Markets">
    Rather than funding mortgages from retail deposits, monolines access capital through institutional investors and government-backed securitization programs like the National Housing Act Mortgage-Backed Securities (NHA MBS) program administered by CMHC. This allows them to fund mortgages at highly competitive rates tied to wholesale capital markets.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Broker Channel Efficiency">
    By relying entirely on mortgage brokers for origination, monolines avoid the cost of a direct sales force. Brokers perform the client-facing work; the monoline focuses exclusively on underwriting, funding, and servicing. The result is a leaner cost structure and, in most cases, a better rate for the end borrower.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Single-Product Focus">
    Without the distraction of cross-selling other financial products, monoline lenders concentrate their underwriting expertise and pricing power entirely on residential mortgages. This focus often produces more consistent, competitive pricing and cleaner product terms.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Monoline Policy Highlights

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Portability and Blend-and-Extend">
    Most monoline lenders support mortgage portability — the ability to transfer your existing rate and terms to a new property when you move. Timelines typically range from 60 to 90 days between the sale of the old property and the purchase of the new one. Many monolines also offer blend-and-extend options, allowing borrowers to add new funds to an existing mortgage at a blended rate rather than fully breaking the term. See the [Porting and Assumption](/lenders/porting-and-assumption) page for complete details.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Limited HELOC Products">
    The absence of a full banking platform is most visible here. Most monolines do not offer readvanceable HELOC products. First National is a notable exception with its Equityline product, but it is not a true readvanceable structure in the same way as bank all-in-one products. Clients who specifically need a readvanceable HELOC — particularly those pursuing the Smith Manoeuvre — should generally look to a Big Bank for that component of their financial plan.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Third-Party Mortgage Servicing">
    Monolines typically do not service their own mortgage portfolios. Day-to-day mortgage administration — payment processing, account inquiries, renewal processing — is handled by third-party servicers. This is transparent to most borrowers but worth understanding: your mortgage may be originated by one entity and serviced by another. Common servicers include First National's own platform, MCAP Service Corporation, and others.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Federally Regulated, OSFI Compliant">
    Monoline lenders are federally regulated financial institutions subject to OSFI guidelines including the B-20 mortgage stress test. They are not a workaround for stress test qualification — they apply the same federal qualifying standards as the Big Banks. Their advantage is rate and penalty structure, not regulatory arbitrage.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

<Note>
  Monoline lenders are **exclusively accessible through licensed mortgage brokers**. You cannot walk into a First National or MCAP office and apply for a mortgage — these institutions do not have public-facing retail channels. If a borrower is not working with a broker, they are by definition locked out of the entire monoline segment of the market, which often contains the most competitive rates available on any given day.
</Note>

<Tip>
  **Monoline IRD calculations are typically more favourable to borrowers than Big Bank calculations.** Most monolines calculate IRD using your actual **contract rate** (the rate you're paying) rather than the bank's inflated posted rate. This means if you need to break a fixed-rate mortgage mid-term with a monoline, the penalty will generally be materially smaller than the equivalent penalty at a Big Bank for the same balance, rate, and remaining term. For clients who have any possibility of needing to break their mortgage early — due to relocation, relationship change, or refinancing — this is a significant financial consideration at the time of lender selection.
</Tip>
